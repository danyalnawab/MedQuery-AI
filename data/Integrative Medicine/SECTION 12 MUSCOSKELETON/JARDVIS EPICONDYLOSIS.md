### **Pathophysiology**

1. **Tissue Affected**:  
   * **LE**: Primarily affects the **extensor carpi radialis brevis** and the **common extensor tendon**.  
   * **ME**: Involves the **wrist flexors**, originating from the medial epicondyle.  
2. **Mechanism of Injury**:  
   * Chronic overuse or trauma leads to **microtearing** and **failed tendon healing**.  
   * Tendons become **weakened, fibrosed, calcified**, and sometimes necrotic due to poor collagen repair.  
   * Early inflammation may contribute to symptoms, but histopathological evidence shows a lack of inflammatory cells in chronic cases.  
3. **Key Changes in Tendons**:  
   * **Disrupted Collagen Organization**: Collagen fibers lose their parallel orientation (Fig. 71.2).  
   * **Tenocyte Hyperplasia**: Increased tenocyte activity suggests chronic degenerative stress.  
   * **Calcification**: In severe cases, tendons exhibit calcified deposits, further reducing functionality.  
4. **Symptoms**:  
   * **LE**: Pain localized over the lateral epicondyle, worsened by gripping or resisted wrist extension.  
   * **ME**: Pain and tenderness near the medial epicondyle, radiating to the ulnar side of the forearm and occasionally the fingers.

---

### **Diagnosis**

1. **Clinical Examination**:  
   * **LE**: Pain elicited by resisted wrist extension, gripping, or palpation just distal to the lateral epicondyle.  
   * **ME**: Pain elicited by resisted wrist flexion, pronation, or palpation near the medial epicondyle.  
   * Both conditions involve reduced grip strength and localized tenderness.  
2. **Differential Diagnosis**:  
   * Rule out cervical radiculopathy, ulnar or radial neuropathy, and joint pathologies.  
3. **Imaging**:  
   * Typically unnecessary unless ruling out other conditions.  
   * MRI or ultrasound may show tendon degeneration in refractory cases.

---

### **Management**

#### **1\. Lifestyle and Conservative Treatment**

* **Rest and Activity Modification**:  
  * Limit repetitive wrist movements (e.g., typing, gripping tools).  
  * Use ergonomic adjustments, such as padded grips or lighter tools.  
* **Ice Therapy**:  
  * Useful in the acute phase (first 7 days) to reduce pain.  
  * Apply for 10 minutes every 4–6 hours using a wet towel for better conduction.  
* **Patient Education**:  
  * Explain the non-inflammatory nature of the condition.  
  * Set realistic expectations: healing may take **3 to 12 months**.

---

#### **2\. Non-Pharmacologic Interventions**

* **Physical Therapy**:  
  * Focus on eccentric strengthening exercises to promote collagen remodeling.  
  * Stretching and mobilization techniques reduce pain and improve function (Fig. 71.4).  
* **Orthotics**:  
  * Elbow straps and wrist splints help offload stress on tendons.  
  * Avoid full immobilization to prevent muscle atrophy.  
* **Acupuncture**:  
  * Provides short-term pain relief by releasing endogenous opioids.  
* **Prolotherapy and PRP**:  
  * **Prolotherapy**: Hypertonic dextrose injections stimulate tendon healing.  
  * **Platelet-Rich Plasma (PRP)**: Delivers growth factors to enhance tissue repair. Effective in chronic, refractory cases.

---

#### **3\. Pharmacologic Management**

* **Nonsteroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drugs (NSAIDs)**:  
  * Topical diclofenac or oral ibuprofen for short-term pain relief.  
  * Avoid prolonged use due to potential gastrointestinal side effects.  
  * **Dosage**:  
    * Topical diclofenac 3% gel: Twice daily for 1–2 weeks.  
    * Oral ibuprofen: 600 mg every 6 hours for no more than 2 weeks.  
* **Corticosteroid Injections**:  
  * Useful for short-term relief (2–6 weeks) in acute pain.  
  * **Dosage**: Methylprednisolone 40 mg in 1 mL lidocaine.  
  * Limit injections to three per year due to risk of tendon rupture or atrophy.

---

#### **4\. Surgical Intervention**

* Reserved for cases with persistent pain despite 6–12 months of conservative treatment.  
* **Procedure**:  
  * Remove degenerated tendon tissue and release affected portions of the tendon.  
* **Outcomes**:  
  * \~85% success rate in case series for pain relief and functional recovery.

---

### **Prevention Strategies**

* Use proper technique during repetitive activities or sports.  
* Avoid gripping with extended arms or extreme wrist flexion.  
* Incorporate regular stretching and strengthening exercises for the wrist and forearm.  
* Evaluate workplace ergonomics to minimize repetitive strain injuries.
