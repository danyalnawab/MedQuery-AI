### **Pathophysiology**

Chronic Fatigue Syndrome (CFS), also referred to as **Systemic Exertion Intolerance Disease (SEID)**, is a multifaceted disorder characterized by profound fatigue, post-exertional malaise, and unrefreshing sleep. Although the precise pathophysiology is not fully understood, several contributing mechanisms have been proposed:

#### **1\. Immune Dysregulation:**

* **Infection as a Trigger**: Common infectious agents, such as Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV), Ross River virus, and Coxiella burnetii, have been associated with CFS onset. Persistent interferon secretion post-infection may contribute to fatigue and achiness.  
* **Hypercoagulability**: Up to 92% of patients exhibit hypercoagulable states, potentially impairing oxygen and nutrient delivery to tissues.

#### **2\. Oxidative Stress and Mitochondrial Dysfunction:**

* Oxidative stress, while evident, may be a consequence rather than a cause. Mitochondrial dysfunction is hypothesized to limit energy production, contributing to fatigue.

#### **3\. Neuroendocrine and Hypothalamic Dysfunction:**

* Dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis disrupts sleep, hormonal balance, and autonomic control. This "energy crisis" may impair the body's ability to meet physiological demands, perpetuating fatigue.

#### **4\. Environmental and Chemical Exposures:**

* Toxins (e.g., pesticides, heavy metals) and stress may increase susceptibility, with evidence of symptom flares following chemical exposures.

#### **5\. Genetic and Psychological Factors:**

* Genetic predisposition and personality traits, such as perfectionism and overachievement, may contribute to stress and immune dysregulation.

---

### **Diagnostic Criteria**

According to the **2015 Institute of Medicine (IOM)** report, the following criteria must be met for a diagnosis of CFS/SEID:

1. **Substantial impairment** in pre-illness occupational, social, or personal activities persisting for \>6 months.  
2. **Fatigue** that is profound, not alleviated by rest, and worsens with exertion.  
3. **Post-exertional malaise** and **unrefreshing sleep** present at least half the time with moderate-to-severe intensity.  
4. At least one of the following:  
   * **Cognitive impairment** (e.g., memory or concentration issues).  
   * **Orthostatic intolerance** (e.g., symptoms worsening with standing).

### **Integrative Management**

Management of CFS requires a **multidimensional approach**, combining lifestyle modifications, nutritional supplementation, pharmaceutical interventions, and mind-body therapies.

---

#### **1\. Lifestyle Modifications**

* **Energy Pacing**: Teach patients to balance activity and rest to avoid post-exertional malaise.  
* **Graded Exercise Therapy (GET)**:  
  * Start with low-intensity activities, such as walking or stretching.  
  * Progress gradually, monitoring for post-exertional symptoms.  
* **Sleep Hygiene**:  
  * Maintain a consistent sleep schedule.  
  * Consider cognitive-behavioral strategies to address insomnia.

---

#### **2\. Nutritional Interventions**

* **Anti-inflammatory Diet**:  
  * Emphasize whole, plant-based foods, healthy fats (omega-3s), and antioxidant-rich fruits and vegetables.  
  * Avoid processed foods, artificial sweeteners (e.g., aspartame), and high-glycemic-load foods.  
* **Hydration**: Encourage ample water intake to support circulation and reduce symptoms of orthostatic intolerance.

---

#### **3\. Supplementation**

##### **To Support Mitochondrial Function:**

* **Carnitine**: 500–1000 mg 2–3 times/day for energy production.  
* **Coenzyme Q10**: 100–300 mg/day to support ATP synthesis.  
* **NADH**: 10–20 mg/day for energy metabolism.  
* **Ribose**: 5 g 2–3 times/day to replenish energy reserves.

##### **To Address Deficiencies:**

* **Vitamin D**: 1000–2000 IU/day; aim for 25(OH)D levels \>30 ng/mL.  
* **Iron**: Supplement if ferritin \<60 ng/mL; monitor levels to avoid overload.  
* **Vitamin B12**: Oral (1 mg/day) or intramuscular (1 mg twice weekly) for fatigue and cognitive symptoms.  
* **Magnesium**: 400–750 mg/day for muscle relaxation and energy production.

##### **To Support Immune and Adrenal Function:**

* **DHEA**: 5–10 mg/day (females) or 25–50 mg/day (males) if DHEA-S levels are low.  
* **Adaptogenic Herbs**:  
  * Rhodiola rosea, Ashwagandha, and Cordyceps for adrenal support and stress resilience.

---

#### **4\. Mind-Body Therapies**

* **Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT)**:  
  * Address maladaptive thought patterns contributing to fatigue and stress.  
* **Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR)**:  
  * Reduces fatigue and improves psychological well-being.  
* **Guided Imagery and Breathing Retraining**:  
  * Alleviates anxiety and enhances relaxation.

---

#### **5\. Pharmaceuticals**

* **Thyroid and Adrenal Support**:  
  * **Thyroid Hormones**: Consider low-dose T4/T3 for suboptimal free T4 levels.  
  * **Hydrocortisone**: 10–20 mg/day for low cortisol levels (\<16 mcg/dL).  
* **Low-Dose Naltrexone (LDN)**:  
  * 1.5–4.5 mg nightly to modulate immune response and reduce inflammation.  
* **Pain and Sleep Management**:  
  * **Pregabalin** or **Duloxetine** for coexisting fibromyalgia or pain.  
  * **Trazodone** or **Melatonin** for sleep disturbances.

---

#### **6\. Emerging and Adjunctive Therapies**

* **Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy (HBOT)**:  
  * Promising in reducing fatigue and improving cognitive symptoms.  
* **Acupuncture and Qigong**:  
  * Enhances energy flow and reduces fatigue through Traditional Chinese Medicine approaches.

---

### **Prevention Prescription**

* **Healthy Lifestyle**:  
  * Regular exercise tailored to tolerance.  
  * Nutritious, anti-inflammatory diet.  
* **Stress Management**:  
  * Practice mindfulness, meditation, or journaling.  
* **Environmental Awareness**:  
  * Minimize exposure to environmental toxins.
